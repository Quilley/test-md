Keep the reading path clear.

Contents, definitions and source links support a longer argument. Beside a wide screen the contents stay in view as a rail; on a narrow one they fold into a disclosure.

What limits completed output?

When work passes through several steps, the busiest machine is not necessarily the step that limits completion. A drink may be prepared yet still wait for a lid, a name or a handoff.

This constructed case has six arrivals, preparation capacity of eight and handoff capacity of four orders per minute. We use capacity to describe a sustained rate, not a waiting time.

Follow work until it is complete.

The simplified path can complete four orders per minute because handoff is the smaller capacity. With six arrivals, unfinished work grows by two orders per minute.1

The model does not tell us where unfinished orders wait. They may accumulate before preparation, on a counter or in a work list. Those possibilities require observations.

The useful output is what reaches the customer.

Test the independent handoff assumption.

A second handoff position may raise the path's capacity if the positions can work independently. Shared staff or counter space could prevent the expected increase. We would measure that relationship before treating the proposal as a decision.1

Evidence boundary. Every rate is constructed for this example. No empirical claim about a real café is supplied.

Glossary

Capacity
The sustained rate a step can complete under the stated assumptions.
Unfinished work
Orders that have arrived but have not completed the path.

Source notes

1. For this serial, constant-rate example, path capacity is the smaller of preparation and handoff capacities: min(8, 4) = 4 orders per minute. Unfinished work grows by 6 − 4 = 2 orders per minute. This is a calculation from constructed inputs. Return to first citation; return to second citation.
