A faster machine can leave a café just as slow. The missing part of the explanation is the handoff.

The visible queue points elsewhere.

Let's say we watch this café for a few minutes. Orders keep arriving, the machine keeps working, and customers keep waiting. The natural guess is that the machine needs to move faster.

But a drink still needs a lid, a name and a person to hand it over. In this model, that last step handles four drinks a minute. Making eight drinks a minute does not give the café eight completed orders a minute.

The useful output is what reaches the customer.

Where the unfinished orders accumulate depends on how the café organizes the work. They might wait before the machine, on the counter, or in a list of orders. The arithmetic tells us that work accumulates. It does not tell us exactly where.

Speed up the wrong step.

Look at the whole path.

This example assumes constant rates, no lost orders and no other limits. A real café also has bursts of arrivals and variation in preparation time. Those would change the waiting experience, so these numbers cannot tell us how long a customer waits.

They do give us a place to look before buying a faster machine: follow a drink until someone can actually take it away.
